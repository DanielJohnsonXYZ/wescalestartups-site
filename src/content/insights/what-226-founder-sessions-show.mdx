I read all 226 public reviews of my mentoring sessions — 406 sessions between 2019 and 2026 — and counted what founders said.

The finding that surprised me was not what they asked about. It was what none of them said. **Not one review out of 226 mentions a funding stage.** No pre-seed. No seed. No Series A. No bootstrapped, no pre-revenue, no bridge.

Funding stage is the axis the entire advisory industry segments on. It is on my own website — "Seed to Series B" — and on the website of nearly every growth consultancy, fractional CMO and agency selling to startups. And it appears exactly zero times in 42,384 characters of founders describing, in their own words, what they needed help with.

Four other things came out of the count, and one of them is the reason to distrust parts of the rest. All of it is below, including the parts that undercut the argument.

## What the data is

226 public reviews, covering 406 mentoring sessions on GrowthMentor between 2019 and 2026, at a 4.94 average. The full corpus, not a sample. Mean review length 188 characters. 208 of the 226 are five-star.

The reviews are public, so the counts are checkable. Word and phrase counts are reproducible by anyone searching the same text. The categorisation of *what problem the founder arrived with* is my own reading of free text — another person coding the same 226 would land near these numbers rather than exactly on them, and I would rather say that than imply a precision the method does not have.

## Finding 1: nobody describes their problem in stage vocabulary

Zero of 226.

The obvious objection is that founders simply know their stage and don't bother saying it. That is true, and it is worth being precise about what it does and doesn't mean. Founders know their stage perfectly well. In the sessions themselves it usually surfaces in the first two minutes as context — the round that closed, the runway remaining — and is then dropped for the rest of the conversation. It never comes back as an explanation of what is wrong.

So the finding is not that stage is unknown or unimportant. It is that **stage is how the industry sorts companies, and not how a growth problem presents itself to the person inside it.** Those are different jobs, and the vocabulary of the first one is close to useless for the second.

This has a practical consequence for anyone selling to founders. A page organised around "growth help for Series A companies" is organised around the buyer's filing category. A page organised around "our pipeline is inconsistent and we don't know why" is organised around the sentence they would actually type. Only one of those matches the demand.

## Finding 2: the most common question is usually not the problem

Of the 126 reviews that name a topic, paid acquisition is the largest single category — 24 mentions. Ads not working, ads not scaling, or which paid channel to try next.

Then: positioning and messaging 14, strategy and fundamentals 13, prioritisation 10, funnel and conversion 10, SEO and content 9, ICP definition 7, measurement and attribution 6, lead generation 5, sales 5, experimentation 5.

The interesting number is a different one. **Sixteen reviews explicitly describe changing their diagnosis during the session** — and most of those describe being moved *off* the channel question. Founders' own words: "the real bottleneck isn't more channels." "The real, hidden issue." "Hiding right under my nose." "We were focused so much on the leadgen."

I run a growth consultancy. The commercial incentive is to sell channel work. The most common thing that actually happens in these conversations is talking someone out of it — usually onto [ICP definition](/insights/define-icp-by-mindset-not-demographics), messaging, or the [conversion step nobody had looked at](/insights/diagnose-growth-bottleneck-before-spend).

## Finding 3: nearly half never name a problem at all

Only 126 of 226 reviews state the problem the founder arrived with. **100 do not.** Forty-four percent describe the session without ever saying what they came to solve. One reviewer says it outright: his own problem statement was, in his words, "pretty broad."

The tempting read is that those reviewers were just writing quickly. I think something more specific is happening, and findings 2 and 3 explain each other.

A channel question is concrete, answerable, and safe to ask a stranger. "I don't know what is wrong with my company" is none of those. So the channel question frequently *is* the broader uncertainty, wearing something more presentable. Which is why the review never names a problem — at the time of booking, there wasn't one to name. There was a feeling that growth was not compounding and a guess about where to look.

If that is right, then the widespread founder experience of "I've tried everything and nothing works" is often not a channel failure at all. It is [a diagnosis that was never made](/insights/random-acts-of-growth), and every subsequent channel decision inherits the error.

## Finding 4: the answer is usually subtraction, and almost nobody describes it that way

Thirty-six reviews mention leaving with a framework, template or resource. Eleven name a concrete next step. Eleven use the word "actionable." Twenty single out directness — "straight to the point", "no fluff", "doesn't pull punches" — which is the most repeated compliment in the corpus after a recommendation.

**Two mention stopping or avoiding anything.** Two, out of 226.

That sits oddly next to finding 2, where the recurring move is talking someone out of the thing they arrived to discuss. The substance of a lot of these conversations is removal: narrow the ICP, turn off the channel that isn't converting, stop selling to four segments at once. But that is not what survives into the review. Founders describe what they were *given*, not what they were *relieved of*, even when the second was the valuable part.

Worth noticing if you have ever finished a strategy session feeling underwhelmed because you left with fewer things to do than you arrived with. That is often the session working.

## Finding 5: almost nobody reports an outcome — which is the limit of all of this

Only 6 of 226 reviews contain any quantified business language at all — revenue, percentages, ROI, conversion rates. **Only 2 describe a result that had already happened.**

This is the finding that constrains the other four. These reviews are written within hours of a conversation, before anything has been implemented. They are a record of how a conversation felt, not of what it caused.

## What this data cannot tell you

Prominently, because the caveats are the reason to trust the rest.

**It is self-selected.** 208 of 226 are five stars, average 4.94. People who had a poor session mostly do not write about it. Every finding above is a finding about *what satisfied reviewers said*, not about founders in general.

**It has a hole in it.** 226 reviews cover 406 sessions. Roughly 180 sessions produced no review and are systematically absent, and there is no reason to assume they resemble the ones that did.

**It measures the session, not the effect.** No claim here about revenue, growth, funding or survival is supportable, and I am not making one.

**Not everyone in it is a founder.** At least 7 are career conversations and around 10 are agency or consultancy operators. Where I have said "founders" I mean the majority of a mixed corpus.

**The time span is uneven.** 2019 to 2026, with 76 reviews from 2021 alone. Some of the channel mix reflects what was fashionable in a given year rather than anything stable.

**The categories are mine.** The regex counts — phrases, outcome language, stage vocabulary — are reproducible. The problem categories in finding 2 are judgement.

## What to do with it

If you are a founder about to hire someone to fix a channel, try writing down what you think the problem is in one sentence first.

If you can't, that is not a preparation failure. On this evidence it is the single most common state a founder arrives in, and it is worth knowing before you spend money on execution. The next step is diagnosis, not delivery: name the constraint from evidence, decide what measure would prove it had moved, and choose the channel last.
